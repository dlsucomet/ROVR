Abstract
Virtual Reality (VR) navigation is typically performed through joystick-based lo-
comotion, which imposes a manual and cognitive burden that can compete with
the primary task the user is trying to perform. Large Language Models (LLMs)
offer an alternative: translating explicit spoken spatial commands into movement
based on the user’s current view, shifting navigation from manual control to ver-
bal intent. However, it remains unclear whether the cognitive load benefits of
LLM-driven voice navigation hold consistently, or whether they depend on the
structural complexity of the environment being navigated. This study compares
joystick-based locomotion against LLM-driven voice navigation across three vir-
tual environments of increasing structural complexity (an open plain, a labyrinth,
a house). We evaluate both modalities using NASA-TLX for perceived cognitive
load and the System Usability Scale (SUS) for usability, and Task Completion
Time (TCT) for navigation efficiency, testing whether environmental complexity
moderates the cognitive load advantage of voice-driven navigation over joystick
locomotion. A Wizard of Oz phase preceded the comparative study to formatively
evaluate the interaction design. We anticipate that the cognitive load advantage
of LLM-driven voice navigation will be more pronounced in structurally complex
environments, where spatial reasoning demands are highest.
Keywords: virtual reality, large language models, cognitive load, VR
navigation, human-computer interaction

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 Background of the Study
VirtualReality(VR)hasemergedasarevolutionarymediumthatenablesusersto
experiencecomputergeneratedenvironmentsasimmersiveandspatiallyembodied
spaces compared to flat visual mediums (?). What sets VR apart is the concept
of presence, which is defined as the psychological sensation of physically exist-
ing within a given environment (?). This perceptual illusion is achieved through
stereoscopic depth cues, real-time head tracking, and sensorimotor contingencies
that align user movement with environmental response (??). The effectiveness
of VR as a medium is closely aligned with theories of embodied cognition, which
states that cognitive processing is grounded in bodily interaction with space and
environment (?).
Despite these immersive qualities, navigation and locomotion remain among
the most persistent usability challenges in VR systems (?). In physical environ-
ments, locomotion is largely automatic and supported by proprioception, vestibu-
lar feedback, and continuous sensorimotor coordination (?). However, physical
movement in VR is often restricted by spatial constraints, resulting in a reliance
on less intuitive artificial locomotion techniques such as joysticks, teleportation,
and walking-in-place systems (?). While these techniques allow for movement at
a greater scale, they introduce a separation between actual physical movement
and the perception of movement (?). Teleportation prevents motion sickness but
disrupts spatial continuity and awareness (?), while joystick-based navigation re-
quires continuous manual input and significant biomechanical coordination, which
can induce visually induced motion sickness (?).

These interaction mechanics significantly increase the extraneous cognitive
load, defined as the mental effort required to manage the complexities of the
interface that are separate from the task itself (?). When locomotion modal-
ities require unnatural manual adjustments and constant attentional resources,
working memory becomes divided (?). To overcome the limitations of handheld
controllers, early voice interfaces were explored. However, traditional rule-based
voice systems often force users to memorize strict command syntaxes, and instead
of reducing the cognitive load, these systems simply shift the burden to forcing
users to recall specific phrasing just to move(?).
Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI), specifically Large Language
Models (LLMs), present a potential way to overcome this challenge by removing
the need for mechanical locomotion or rule based voice interaction (?). LLMs
are large-scale neural architectures trained on extensive text, capable of advanced
contextual reasoning, intent inference, and structured output generation from
naturallanguageinput(?). Thesemodelscantranslatehigh-levelintentexpressed
in natural language into specific spatial commands, effectively functioning as real-
time semantic analyzers (?)
Recent works have explored integrating LLMs directly into immersive systems
to improve locomotion. ? and ? demonstrated the feasibility of integrating AI-
driven assistants in VR for executing interactive task. Specifically, ? showed that
users could navigate virtual environments through context-aware spoken com-
mands with usability comparable to traditional teleportation methods. ? pro-
posed NavAI, a generalizable framework for mapping natural language instruc-
tions to spatial actions within VR environments. Together, these studies show a
clear move away from physical controllers and toward voice-based interaction.
This study therefore investigates whether environmental complexity moder-
ates the cognitive load advantage of LLM-driven voice navigation over traditional
joystick-based locomotion. Users navigate virtual environments of increasing
structural complexity using two modalities: joystick-based movement and LLM-
driven voice commands. In doing so, this research examines whether the benefits
of natural language navigation observed in prior work hold consistently as spatial
complexityincreases, orwhethertheydiminishorintensifyundermoredemanding
navigational conditions.

1.2 Research Objectives
BuildingonthecognitivefrictionassociatedwithtraditionalVRnavigationparadigms,
the primary objective of this research is to investigate how an LLM-driven
voice modality compares to standard controller-based locomotion in
supporting spatial exploration. Specifically, it seeks to answer the following
research question:
How does an LLM-driven voice navigation modality compare
to traditional joystick locomotion in supporting spatial ex-
ploration across virtual environments of varying complexity?
To address this main objective, a proof-of-concept LLM-driven voice naviga-
tion system (ROVR) will be developed and evaluated against traditional locomo-
tion mechanics. The specific objectives of the study are:
1. To define the natural language interaction patterns and spatial referencing
constraints required to for an LLM-driven voice navigation system.
2. To develop the proof-of-concept system and integrate both voice and con-
troller modalities across three virtual environments of varying complexity.
3. To evaluate the effectiveness of the voice-driven modality against traditional
locomotion by comparing task completion time, cognitive load, and system
usability across the varying testing conditions.
1.3 Scope and Limitations of the Research
We first focus on designing the system interaction for an LLM-driven voice navi-
gation framework by identifying natural language patterns and spatial referencing
strategies. The scope of this phase is limited to a Wizard of Oz (WoZ) pilot study,
which established the foundational interaction model for users exploring a virtual
environment from a first-person perspective using only their voice. A primary lim-
itation of this design phase is that the vocabulary and phrasing were derived from
a specific, small group of participants during the pilot test. Consequently, these
captured language patterns may not fully represent how the broader population
naturally speaks to or interacts with a VR navigation system.
3
--- PAGE 15 ---
The ROVR prototype will then be developed in Unity for the Meta Quest
headset, enabling users to navigate three virtual environments of increasing com-
plexity: an open plain, a house, and a maze. The system utilizes a localized
Large Language Model (LLM) to translate spoken commands into precise move-
ment vectors based strictly on the user’s current line of sight. A major limitation
of this development is that the system is strictly limited to processing English
language commands. Furthermore, the LLM acts purely as a navigational guide;
it is not programmed to manipulate virtual objects, trigger environmental inter-
actions, control user interface menus, or allow the user to move through solid
walls.
We will evaluate the differences in task completion time, cognitive load, and
perceived system usability between ROVR and traditional handheld controller-
based navigation. The scope of this evaluation involves a between-subjects ex-
periment where participants complete navigation tasks across the three test envi-
ronments. The study specifically evaluates outcomes using the exact instruments
designed for these metrics: The amount of time it takes for users to complete the
tasks (Task Completion Time), cognitive load is assessed using the NASA Task
Load Index (NASA-TLX), and system usability is evaluated using the System
Usability Scale (SUS). A limitation of this evaluation is the inherent subjectivity
of the self-reported surveys, as well as potential individual differences in spatial
reasoning abilities across the distinct participant groups in the between-subjects
design.
1.4 Significance of the Research
Virtual Reality represents the frontier of visual interaction; however, the steep
learning curve of traditional hardware controllers remains a significant barrier to
widespread adoption and user presence. By proposing a transition from “manual
tools” toward “natural interaction metaphors,” this study provides meaningful
contributions to Human-Computer Interaction (HCI).
Technologically, this research demonstrates the viability of utilizing localized
LargeLanguageModels(LLMs)forzero-latencyspatialnavigation. Byaddressing
the network latency issues inherent in cloud-based frameworks, this study sets a
precedent for developing highly responsive, privacy-preserving AI assistants in
virtual environments.
This study provides empirical evidence on the cognitive cost of existing navi-
gation techniques within VR interaction. By demonstrating that natural language

2.1 Locomotion Modalities in VR
Locomotion is a fundamental component of user interaction in virtual environ-
ments, enabling users to navigate digital spaces beyond their physical boundaries.
Researchers broadly categorize locomotion techniques into walking-based (e.g.,
redirected walking, omnidirectional treadmills), steering-based (e.g., joysticks,
gaze-directedmovement),selection-based(e.g.,teleportation),manipulation-based
(e.g., world-pulling), and automated approaches (???). In commercial VR de-
ployments, however, the landscape has largely converged around joystick-based
steering and teleportation (?).
Each established modality presents distinct trade-offs across physical demand,
spatial awareness, and cognitive effort (??). Teleportation eliminates continuous
motion to protect users from motion sickness, but it disrupts continuous spa-
tial updating, degrading cognitive map formation and spatial orientation (??).
Conversely, joystick-based continuous locomotion affords fluid directional control
and spatial continuity, but creates sustained optic flow without corresponding
vestibular stimulation. According to Sensory Conflict Theory, this neuro-sensory
mismatch—where the visual system perceives movement that the vestibular sys-
tem does not detect—is the primary driver of cybersickness, manifesting as nau-
sea, oculomotor fatigue, and disorientation (???). Manual thumbstick controls
compound this issue during rotation; abrupt continuous turning or erratic tra-
jectory corrections produce peripheral visual instability that heightens discomfort
(??). This discomfort is particularly pronounced when movement mechanics in-
duce the ”rollercoaster effect”—a phenomenon where rapid, unmediated accelera-
tion, sharp directional shifts, or variable speeds subject the user’s visual system to
6
--- PAGE 18 ---
intense dynamic velocity changes without physical inertial feedback (??). Voice-
based steering offers a potential alternative by generating smoother, predictable
linear motion vectors that eliminate sudden velocity spikes and minimize trajec-
tory shifts (??), yet its comparison against manual control remains underexplored
in scalable contexts (?).
In this study, we systematically evaluate how locomotion cognitive load and
usability costs vary with environmental complexity, comparing standard manual
joystick-basedlocomotiondirectlyagainstourproposedLLM-enabledvoiceframe-
work. To isolate the mechanics of interaction and ensure a fair comparison across
modalities, we exclude selection-based teleportation. Instead, both modalities are
constrained to identical directional controls: continuous linear translation (mov-
ing forward, backward, or vertically) and discrete angular rotation (shifting the
view vector in fixed 90-degree increments).
Whilecybersicknessisnotevaluatedasaformaldependentvariableinourmet-
ric set, its underlying physiological triggers serve as a core consideration in our ex-
perimental procedure and technical implementation. To mitigate the rollercoaster
effect, safeguard participant comfort, and prevent severe sickness symptoms from
confounding cognitive performance, our system design eliminates variable linear
accelerationinfavoroffixed-velocitytranslationcaps,enforcesconstantmovement
speeds, and utilizes fixed discrete snap-turns rather than unconstrained contin-
uous yaw or pitch rotation. Procedurally, participant exposure is constrained
through managed trial durations, structured rest breaks between conditions, and
immediate stop protocols upon any self-reported onset of discomfort.

2.2 Immersive Interaction in VR
Virtual reality differentiates itself from conventional display mediums not only
through visual fidelity, but through its capacity to produce a psychological sense
of spatial presence, the feeling of physically existing within a computer-generated
environment (??). This sense of presence is sustained by the system’s ability
to respond to the user’s physical movements in real time, creating the percep-
tual illusion that actions in the virtual world are a direct extension of actions in
the physical one (?). Critically, presence is not a passive perceptual effect; it is
grounded in embodied cognition, the principle that cognitive processing is shaped
by the engagement of the body within a space (??). ? formalizes this through the
Sense of Embodiment, which comprises three components: self-location, agency,
and body ownership. Together, these components describe not only where a user
perceives themselves to be but also how much control they feel over their actions
within that space. This distinction matters for interaction design: a VR system
that disrupts agency, by demanding unnatural physical gestures or sustained at-
tention to interface mechanics, undermines the very embodiment that makes VR
effective as a medium.
Despite this, research on VR interaction has focused on locomotion mechan-
ics, benchmarking techniques against each other on measures of comfort, sickness,
and navigation efficiency (???). This focus is understandable given that locomo-
tion is the most basic prerequisite for spatial engagement, but it has come at a
cost. Interaction in VR is broader than movement: it encompasses how users ori-
ent themselves, maintain spatial awareness, form cognitive maps of environments,
and direct their attention between the task at hand and the mechanics of getting
around. These demands do not operate independently of locomotion; they com-
pete with it for the same limited cognitive resources (??). Reducing interaction
research to locomotion comparisons leaves this competition largely unexamined.
The deeper issue is the paradigm mismatch. The dominant input devices in
VR, handheld controllers and joysticks, were inherited from desktop and gaming
conventions designed for flat two-dimensional interaction spaces (??). Mapping
these inputs onto three-dimensional spatial environments introduces an abstrac-
tion layer between user intent and environmental response. In simple environ-
ments, this abstraction is manageable. However, as environmental complexity
increases, the cost of maintaining that abstraction layer subsequently compounds:
users must simultaneously manage navigation mechanics, spatial orientation, and
task execution, each drawing on the same working memory resources. The friction
this produces is not incidental to the interface, but a structural consequence of
applying a 2D interaction paradigm to a 3D spatial medium.

2.3 Cognitive Demands of VR Navigation
Thefrictionintroducedbyartificiallocomotionmanifestsmostacutelyinwayfind-
ing. Wayfinding is the cognitive process that directs movement rather than move-
ment itself, encompassing the recall of a destination, the construction or con-
sultation of a mental map, route planning, and ongoing monitoring of progress
toward a goal (???). Each of these steps draws on working memory. In a virtual
environment, the user must perform this process while simultaneously operating
an unfamiliar locomotion interface, so the two compete for the same finite cogni-
tive resources (??). The effort spent managing the mechanics of movement is an
extraneous cognitive load, mental effort directed at the interface rather than the
task it is intended to serve (?).
Controller-based navigation adds a further source of cognitive burden through
the physical mechanics of operating the interface. Operating handheld controllers
requires continuous manual engagement: gripping the device, manipulating joy-
sticks to steer. In the case of teleportation, the driver is repeatedly directed and
confirmed each jump. This manual engagement is sustained for the entire dura-
tion of a navigation task, requiring ongoing attentional monitoring that draws on
the same working memory resources as wayfinding (??). Rather than functioning
as an independent, additive cost, the physical effort of manual control is one fur-
ther channel through which locomotion competes with the user’s limited cognitive
resources.
This burden is further compounded by the loss of spatial orientation. Artificial
locomotion techniques remove the continuous self-motion cues that keep people
oriented during natural movement. Teleportation, by transporting the user in-
stantly, removes these cues completely and disrupts the spatial updating needed
to maintain a sense of place (?). However, the severity of disorientation depends
heavily on the environment. ? found that disorientation was greatest in a fea-
tureless open field and least in a structured environment containing boundaries
and landmarks, indicating that orientation is not governed by the locomotion
technique alone, but by the spatial cues available by the environment.
These findings converge on a more fundamental point, that the cost of navi-
gation is not an intrinsic property of a locomotion technique but varies with the
environmentinwhichthattechniqueisused. Sparseenvironmentsdepriveusersof
the cues needed to stay oriented, while structurally dense environments multiply
the demands of route planning and mental map maintenance. ? demonstrate this
directly, reporting that navigation performance declines and physiological stress
increases as environments become more complex, while supportive environmental
cues reduce that load. These costs are not merely subjective, as the same condi-

task completion times, navigation errors, and failed attempts to reach a goal (??).

2.4 Voice-Based Interaction in VR
To resolve the interaction friction of manual controllers, researchers have inves-
tigated alternative, non-manual modalities, yet many introduce secondary chan-
nel conflicts. Gaze-based steering, for instance, suffers from the classic Midas
touch problem: because the eyes are simultaneously used for environmental in-
spection and issuing movement commands, systems struggle to distinguish passive
visual scanning from active navigational intent (??). Gesture-based interaction
and posture-leaning (e.g., tilting the torso to steer) reintroduce physical friction
through sustained mid-air movements or isometric muscular strain, leading to
rapid muscular fatigue (??). Voice-based interaction avoids these failure modes
entirely. By operating as an auditory and linguistic modality, speech competes
with neither the visual channel required for spatial orientation nor the user’s
physical endurance, making it a uniquely suited modality for spatial navigation
in virtual environments (??).
Early efforts to deploy speech for VR navigation demonstrated clear feasibil-
ity, particularly in structured domains. For example, ? introduced a voice-driven
system for virtual museum tours that allowed users—especially those with mo-
tor impairments—to navigate to specific exhibits using semantic commands. The
system relied on an ontological reasoning framework paired with a rule-based
grammar to interpret spoken requests. Similarly, ? evaluated speech-driven loco-
motion in virtual training scenarios across voice-only, voice-and-gaze, and hybrid
conditions, establishing that speech could successfully replace physical inputs for
targeted movement tasks.
However, these early systems were fundamentally constrained by their reliance
on rigid rule-based grammars and predefined template structures. Systems utiliz-
ing discrete semantic grammars require users to translate spatial intentions into
fixed command templates (e.g., ”Move Forward 5 Meters”) or select from pre-
defined destination names (??). What these approaches share is not a failure of
voice as an input channel, but a structural limitation in command processing.
Rule-based voice systems merely relocate interaction friction rather than remove
it—shifting the burden from the physical hands operating a controller to the cog-
nitive memory required to recall exact syntax and allowed vocabulary (?). Users
of rule-based speech interfaces report this demand directly, highlighting command
rejections caused by minor phrasing deviations as a primary source of friction (?).
Addressing this limitation requires transitioning from rigid command parsing
to unconstrained natural language interpretation—a capability enabled directly
by modern large language models. Our study introduces an LLM-driven voice
framework evaluated directly against standard joystick-based locomotion, inten-

relies on restrictive rule-based navigation that forces users to memorize command

input. This research aims to advance LLM-driven voice navigation frameworks
and establish their broader efficacy in reducing cognitive load, minimizing task
completion time, and maximizing usability across virtual environments of increas-
ing structural complexity.

2.5 Large Language Models (LLMs) for VR Nav-
igation
Large language models address the structural limitations of rule-based voice sys-
tems directly. Rather than matching spoken input against a rigid grammar, an
LLM interprets natural language by drawing on contextual information, allowing
users to express movement commands in free-form speech without having to learn
a predefined vocabulary or command structure (??). This removes the memoriza-
tion burden identified in rule-based systems at its source, transforming voice from
an awkward command utility into an organic extension of spatial intent.
This capability has rapidly transitioned from a theoretical proposal into work-
ing interactive frameworks. ? developed an open-source package for embedding
LLM-powered conversational agents directly into XR applications, demonstrating
that real-time LLM integration is technically feasible within consumer VR hard-
ware constraints. ? similarly demonstrated LLM-powered assistance for interac-
tive task execution in industrial environments, proving that translating language
into action within a 3D space generalizes effectively across complex practical con-
texts.
The foundational precedents for integrating spatial-language mapping into in-
teractive VR frameworks are established by ?, who developed NavAI as a gen-
eralizable layer for converting natural language into spatial actions, and ?, who
conducted a direct empirical comparison between controller-based teleportation,
rule-based voice steering, and LLM-driven locomotion using NASA-TLX, SUS,
presence, and cybersickness measures. In their study, the LLM-driven condition
achievedusability, presence, andcybersicknessscorescomparabletoteleportation,
while significantly improving user attention within the environment. This demon-
strated that prompt-driven, natural language movement does not introduce steep
interactive penalties even as it removes the cognitive overhead of syntax recall,
establishing it as a viable locomotion modality in its own right.
However, existing implementations remain fundamentally bounded. While
? and ? successfully leveraged LLMs to eliminate fixed commands, their eval-
uation designs focused heavily on static geometric commands and paired them
with discrete teleportation mechanics. Furthermore, their empirical insights are
derived from a single testing environment of fixed, static complexity. Whether
the performance and experiential advantages of an LLM-driven architecture hold,
strengthen, or narrow as virtual environments grow more structurally demanding
remains an open question.

3.1 Semantic-Geometric Division of Labor
The integration of voice user interfaces (VUIs) within virtual reality (VR) envi-
ronments fundamentally disrupts traditional user interaction design by replacing
explicit physical inputs with conversational primitives (?). Instead of relying on
predefined graphical menus or button mapping, the interface leverages the open-
ended nature of speech to mediate the user’s presence and actions.
Historically, user interaction within spatial computing environments has been
bound to manual, device-driven locomotion loops, where users manipulate joy-
sticks, trackpads, or spatial hand tracking to negotiate 3D space (??). While
these mechanical systems provide deterministic control, they force the user to op-
erate at a low-level execution layer, requiring them to constantly calculate spatial
vectors and coordinate transformations in their working memory. As established
by cognitive load theory (?), this constant translation of intent into fine motor
corrections increases the user’s extraneous cognitive load, distracting from the
primary task within the immersive space.
Developing a voice-driven, large language model (LLM) navigation system re-
quires shifting this paradigm from low-level mechanical execution to high-level
semantic orchestration (?). Within this paradigm, the interface must accommo-
date unconstrained natural language, which is inherently non-linear, ambiguous,
and deeply tied to the user’s perception of their virtual environment (?).
Human-computerinteraction(HCI) modelsestablish that whenusers arefreed
from rigid syntax constraints, their spatial instructions naturally manifest across

--- PAGE 33 ---
a spectrum of fuzzy spatial descriptions, egocentric frames of reference, and real-
time continuous linguistic feedback (?).
Traditional VR relies on device-driven loops that force users to calculate spa-
tial vectors manually. This study addresses this by implementing a Semantic-
Geometric Division of Labor within the ROVR prototype, a design paradigm
supported by recent architectural frameworks that explicitly decouple high-level
semanticintentfromlow-levelgeometricconstraintstopreventcognitiveandcom-
putational bottlenecks (??).
Specifically, ROVR implements a low-latency, voice-based interaction system
that embeds this division of labor directly within the Unity engine workspace. By
utilizing an integrated LLM to handle real-time semantic processing, the system
abstracts the underlying geometric complexity away from the user. The LLM
interprets the user’s high-level intent, unconstrained phrasing, and spatial de-
scriptions, translating them directly into precise coordinate transformations and
pathfinding instructions within Unity. Consequently, the user is completely un-
burdened from calculating spatial vectors or managing physical inputs, allowing
the system’s architecture to seamlessly bridge the gap between human language
and spatial geometry.
3.2 Linguistic Modalities in Locomotion
Empirical evidence in spoken dialogue design indicates that users do not inter-
act with spatial computing platforms through a single linguistic style; instead,
they fluidly navigate across a spectrum of spatial specificity, varying their com-
mands based on prior technical conditioning and perceived cognitive load (??).
Through our open-ended Wizard of Oz (WoZ) methodology, we observed that un-
constrained user inputs naturally manifest across three distinct interaction modal-
ities:
• Geometric Commands: This modality represents micro-level, granular
instructionswheretheuserdictatesspecificdirectionalvectors, unitsofmea-
surement, or relative directional shifts (e.g., “move forward 5 meters,” “go
left by 10 meters,” or “down a bit”). As noted by human-computer in-
teraction (HCI) frameworks on system legacy (?), users frequently default
to geometric commands when initially introduced to an unfamiliar voice
interface. This behavior occurs because traditional desktop and console
paradigms have conditioned users to act as human calculators, assuming
that computers can only interpret deterministic, low-level metrics. In this

centric point-of-view (POV) to establish logical environmental triggers (e.g.,
“move forward until you touch the door” or “turn right at the next wall”).
This behavior directly reflects theories of perceptual-action loops and em-
bodied cognition (??), where spatial navigation is tightly coupled with real-
time feedback from immediate structural boundaries. Local reactive com-
mands successfully offload the cognitive burden of distance estimation onto
the system without requiring global environment maps.

organize cognitive maps around prominent, non-visible landmarks. When
an interaction model permits unconstrained speech, users rapidly abandon
granular vector commands as soon as they believe the system understands
the broader spatial context, offloading the global pathfinding task entirely.
True global semantic navigation requires an omniscient database where de-
velopers must pre-catalog, tag, and ingest exhaustive global scene metadata to
constantly feed it into a Large Language Model (LLM) context window (?). At-

data grounding, as every individual object, room, and landmark within the envi-

the Unity spatial hierarchy and the LLM’s semantic knowledge base.

3.3 Human-Centered Spatial Orchestration
3.3.1 Biomechanical Embodiment and Gaze-Ground Pro-
jection
A critical paradigm governing spatial voice interfaces is the psychological concept
of embodiment. In virtual environments, the sense of embodiment represents the
degree to which a user perceives a virtual viewport or avatar as their own physical
body (?). Crucially, when users navigate a first-person 3D virtual environment
via an unconstrained microphone channel, they do not perceive the viewport as a
disembodied, weightless camera drone or a floating software point. Instead, even
in the complete absence of a visible virtual avatar mesh, users actively project an
24
--- PAGE 36 ---
implicit, gravity-bound biomechanical mental model onto the interface (?).
This embodied projection manifests through the spontaneous use of human
biomechanical verbs and action schemas (e.g., “jump,” “bend forward a bit,” or
“look down”),alongsidestrictexpectationsofterrestriallocomotion. Intraditional
spatial development environments like the Unity engine, a raw camera tracking
loop moves dynamically along a 3D view vector; consequently, if a user looks
upward and triggers a forward translation, the camera flies vertically into space.
Humans, however, fundamentally reject this disembodied mechanic. If a user tilts
their head upward to observe a high-altitude target and commands the system to
“move forward,” theyexpecthorizontaltranslationrestrictedtothegroundplane,
retaining a stabilized bipedal posture rather than experiencing uncommanded
vertical flight.
Furthermore, users naturally couple their gaze to their voice commands, utiliz-
ing egocentric references like “move towards what I am looking at.” This indicates
that a spatial computing architecture cannot treat natural language as an isolated
text stream.
To prevent the severe visual-vestibular sensory conflicts and resultant cyber-
sickness associated with uncoupled gaze-directed steering (??), ROVR synthe-
sizes linguistic intent with real-time head-mounted display (HMD) orientation
data. The architecture strictly maps forward locomotion intent onto a 2D hor-
izontal plane (X,Z coordinates), ensuring the virtual body remains grounded
unless vertical translation (Y coordinate) is explicitly commanded.
3.3.2 Closed-World Vision
To maintain an intuitive, cognitively coherent interaction loop that matches hu-
man perceptual boundaries, the ROVR architecture enforces a strict Closed-
World Assumption upon the Large Language Model’s (LLM) spatial reasoning
matrix. Because human spatial cognition is dynamically coupled to immediate
visual perception, human navigational logic is inherently bounded by the user’s
active Field of View (FOV) and real-time lines of sight (?). A user cannot form an
intenttonavigatetowardorinteractwithanenvironmentalentitythatisoccluded
or structurally isolated from their present perspective.
To mirror this cognitive limitation, the integrated LLM does not operate as an
omniscient global planner. Instead, its knowledge base is dynamically throttled
to match the user’s visual footprint. By implementing continuous physics-based
raycasting within the Unity engine workspace, the system establishes a precise

7.1.1 Participant Recruitment
The evaluation study will recruit 30 participants (N = 30) through convenience
and purposive sampling methods within the university community. All partici-
pantsmetthestrictinclusioncriteria: beingatleast18yearsofageandpresenting
no known linguistic processing difficulties, or severe uncorrected visual deficits.
Prior to commencing the evaluation, each individual received a standardized
briefing regarding the study’s overall goals, the nature of the tasks, and gen-
eral safety procedures within immersive environments. Informed consent was for-
mally obtained from all participants prior to testing, in strict compliance with
institutional and university ethical protocols. This onboarding process explicitly
guaranteed voluntary participation, comprehensive data anonymization, and the
unrestricted right to withdraw from the experiment at any point without penalty.
7.1.2 Experimental Design and Tasks
To evaluate how both locomotion modalities respond to scaling environmental
friction, participants were required to execute specific, goal-directed tasks across
three progressively complex virtual environments, reflecting our established envi-
ronmental complexity gradient:
1. World 1 (Plain World): Serving as a sterile control environment stripped
of physical barriers and semantic metadata, this stage isolated baseline lo-
comotive comfort and input fidelity. Participants were instructed to per-
form basic, low-level spatial calibration maneuvers: executing discrete lat-
eral translations (move left, move right), vertical adjustments (move up,
move down), and directional orientation shifts (turn around).
2. World 2 (Labyrinth World): Introducing high geometric friction and
structural constraints without semantic objects, this environment tested
manual pathfinding endurance and spatial cognition under stress. Partic-
ipants were required to successfully navigate from a standardized starting
position to a designated visual finish zone at the exit of a complex, winding
maze structure.
3. World 3 (House World): Introducing high semantic complexity and re-
alistic contextual constraints, this environment evaluated ecological validity.
Bounded strictly by core spatial metadata abstractions (doors, walls, and
chairs), participants were tasked with executing a multi-room exploratory
checklist, which required them to sequentially visit and verify all primary

7.1.3 Evaluation Metrics and Instruments
In accordance with our core evaluation principle of empirical triangulation, sys-
tem performance and user experience were evaluated across a balanced matrix of
subjective, objective, and qualitative diagnostic lenses:
• Cognitive Workload (NASA-TLX): The subjective cognitive load im-
posed by each locomotion modality was captured using the standardized
NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) multi-item questionnaire. This in-
strumentevaluatedworkloadacrosssixkeysubscales: mentaldemand,phys-
ical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration level,
allowing us to explicitly measure whether ROVR successfully achieves cog-
nitive offloading.
• System Usability (SUS): General interface adoption, learning curve fric-
tion, and structural usability were benchmarked using the standard 10-item
System Usability Scale (SUS) (?). This metric was deployed post-test to
evaluatetheperceivedcomplexityandoperationalconfidenceofvoice-driven
steering relative to industrial-standard hardware.
• Simulator Sickness (SSQ): Given the divergent locomotion models under
comparison, the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) (?) was adminis-
tered before and after each session to quantify changes in nausea, oculo-
motor discomfort, and disorientation across conditions, and to confirm that
observed usability and workload differences were not confounded by differ-
ential discomfort.
• Operational Efficiency (Task Completion Time): Task Completion
Time (TCT) was gathered electronically as our primary objective metric.
Measured in seconds via automated internal Unity engine log modules, TCT
tracked the exact duration from a task’s initiation trigger to its structural

7.1.4 Think-Aloud Protocol
Participants in the control group were asked to follow a concurrent “think-aloud”
protocol, verbalizing their reasoning and intentions while completing each naviga-
tional task. These utterances were audio-recorded and later transcribed. Because
the ROVR condition inherently produces a comparable verbal record through
its voice-command interface, the two utterance sets were analyzed side by side
to examine differences in verbal expression strategies, hesitation patterns, and
error-recovery language across conditions.
7.1.5 Data Analysis Plan
Quantitative measures (SUS, NASA-TLX subscales, SSQ scores, and TCT) will
be compared between the control and experimental groups using independent-
samples t-tests, with non-parametric alternatives applied where normality as-
sumptions are violated.
Beyond these summary statistics, navigation logs from both conditions will be
analyzed for recurring patterns and behavioral trends, including command repe-
tition, backtracking, hesitation pauses, and error-recovery sequences. These pat-
terns will be compared against the usability issues surfaced during the Wizard-of-
Oz workshop described in Section 4.2, to determine whether previously identified
friction points persist in the finalized prototype or have been resolved. Think-
aloud and voice-command transcripts are analyzed alongside the debriefing inter-
views to form a single qualitative account of how each locomotion modality was
experienced across the three environments.
